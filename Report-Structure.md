# Report Structure

The report is written **for a fellow technical interaction designer**. It is not a
scientific report — no citations, no theory. Just your solutions, and why they are the
ones you chose.

It should also stand on its own: assume the examiner is reading it on a plane with no
network. Anything essential has to be *in* the document, not only behind a link.

## Sections

- **Title page** — project name, team members, names and emails.

- **Project description** (one page) — what you set out to achieve, for whom, and the
  one twist that makes it yours rather than a generic to-do.

- **The user study** — your population, how many people you spoke to, the themes that
  came out of it, and the prioritised tasks you derived. The deliverable you handed in,
  as it ended up rather than as it started.

- **Wireframes** (a few pages) — the essential screens. Link to the originals if you
  like, but the main screens belong in the report itself.

- **Usability testing results** (one or two pages) — what you tested, what it told you,
  and what you changed because of it.

- **Visual design and accessibility** (one or two pages) — the design language you chose
  and why, plus the accessibility audit and what it made you fix.

- **Implementation**
  - Link to the GitHub repository
  - Link to your Back4App database — invite the examiner accounts listed on learnIT
  - An ER diagram of your data model
  - How the examiners run your code. Normally your repo's `README.md` already explains
    this. But if anything secret is needed — the contents of an `.env` file, app keys,
    configuration that is deliberately not committed — put it in the report.
  - If you have created accounts for the examiners to log in with, say so and give the
    credentials. (This makes sense for some applications and not others.)

- **Reflection** (two pages max) — looking back at the project:
  - What worked well?
  - What could have been done better — by the team, and by the TID staff?
