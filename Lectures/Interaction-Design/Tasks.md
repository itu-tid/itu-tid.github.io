# Tasks

Interviews give you what people said. Thematic analysis gives you what keeps coming up. Neither of those is something an app can do. **Tasks** are the step in between: they turn a finding into something you could build, and they are what the user-study deliverable asks you to hand in.

The word comes from Søren Lauesen, who uses it in a specific sense worth learning.

## The coffee test

> A task is something that, once you have finished it, you can go and have a coffee.

That is the whole definition, and it does more work than it looks. It rules out almost everything people write down when asked for requirements.

**Tasks:**

- Do this week's shopping for the flat
- Get the kids' packing list ready the night before a trip
- Work out what still needs doing before the guests arrive

**Not tasks:**

| what it is | why it fails | example |
|---|---|---|
| a click | you cannot stop there | "tap the checkbox" |
| a screen | screens are your answer, not their need | "the settings page" |
| a feature | that is a solution in disguise | "push notifications" |
| a whole life goal | too big to finish, ever | "be more organised" |

The middle two are the ones you will get wrong. A task describes what someone is trying to accomplish **before they have ever seen your app** — so if you could not have written it down after the interview, without knowing what you were going to build, it is not a task yet.

## What one looks like

Four fields. Keep it short; a task that runs to a page is two tasks.

**Name** — a verb phrase, in the user's words, not yours.

**Trigger** — what starts it. Usually a moment in the day or an event, not a decision to open an app. This is the field people skip and it is the one that tells you when your app has to be reachable, and on what.

**Sub-steps** — what happens, in order, as it happens now. Still no app: you are recording reality, not designing.

**Variants** — what happens when it does not go straightforwardly. This is where the design work actually lives. Most apps handle the happy path and fall apart here.

### Worked example

> **Name.** Do this week's shopping for the flat.
>
> **Trigger.** Someone notices the milk has run out, or it is Saturday morning.
>
> **Sub-steps.**
> 1. Work out what is missing — from memory, and by looking in the fridge
> 2. Ask the others whether they need anything
> 3. Write it down somewhere, on paper or in a phone note
> 4. Go to the shop
> 5. Cross things off while walking around
> 6. Notice, at the till, the thing that was forgotten
>
> **Variants.**
> - Someone else has already bought half of it
> - A flatmate remembers something after you have left the flat
> - The shop does not have it, and you need to know whether a substitute is acceptable
> - Two people go shopping on the same day without knowing

Look at what falls out of that. Step 2 and the first variant are the entire argument for sharing a list rather than owning one. Step 5 says the app has to work one-handed, in a shop, on a phone. The second variant says changes have to arrive on someone else's phone while they are out. None of that came from a feature list — it came from writing down what already happens.

That is why this is worth the afternoon it takes.

## Prioritising

You will end up with more tasks than you can build. Rank them, and say why. Two questions are usually enough:

- **How often does it happen?** Daily beats monthly.
- **How badly does it go now?** A task people already manage fine is a poor place to spend your one twist.

Frequent and painful goes first. Rare and painful is where the interesting design problems live, but it is not where you start. Frequent and fine is a trap: it looks like a big win and changes nothing.

## Tasks, not user stories

You have probably met *user stories* — "As a flatmate, I want to share a list, so that we do not buy milk twice." They are not wrong, and industry uses them everywhere. **This course says tasks**, for one reason: a user story has the solution built into it. The sentence above already decided there is a shared list. A task does not decide anything — it records what someone is trying to do, and leaves you free to find out that the answer was not a shared list at all.

Use the word *task* in your hand-in, so we are all talking about the same object.

## What to hand in

- Your population, and how many people you interviewed
- Three to five themes, each with a quote that earned it
- Each theme mapped to at least one task, in the four fields above
- The tasks, prioritised, with a sentence on why that order

Feedback comes back a couple of weeks later, with time to act on it before wireframing starts — and the wireframing lecture assumes you have. The syllabus has the dates.
